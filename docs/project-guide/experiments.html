Validation studies

Experiments A & B

Two separate 30-build synthetic cohorts exist purely to validate the SPC and capability machinery under known conditions, since the real telemetry/history/ only has 4 builds so far — not nearly enough to prove any statistical method works. Both are generated by seeded, reproducible code and independently verified by dedicated scripts.

Experiment A — SPC Baseline Methodology

Generator: scripts/generate-experiment.ts + src/telemetry/experimentGenerator.ts, seed 20260815. Output: 30 records in telemetry/experiments/spc-validation-cohort/ (build-exp-001.json … build-exp-030.json). Verified by scripts/verify-experiment.ts (52 checks).

Its purpose is stated directly in the generator's header comment: evaluate EXPANDING_HISTORY versus TRAILING_WINDOW SPC analysis across a deliberately changing process, across five defined phases, walk-forward (at build N, the analysis never sees build N+1 or later).

MethodPASSWARNFAILTotal
EXPANDING_HISTORY132630

Both methods fail most builds in this cohort — that's expected and by design: Phase A starts noisy and high, and the cohort is intentionally built to exercise every gate reason, not to look like a healthy real project.

Why Rule 1 Became "Stuck"

Beginner explanation

Build 1's score (64) was genuinely unusual back when the process was noisy. But once Phase C settled into a much quieter, lower rhythm, the statistics recalculated from all of history suddenly see that same old build-1 score as an extreme outlier all over again — even though nothing happened to build 1 itself. Because expanding history never forgets anything, that one old point keeps re-triggering "beyond 3-sigma" for the rest of the cohort, purely because the goalposts moved under it.

Technical explanation

src/spc/experimentAnalysis.ts documents the mechanism directly: "as later, lower-variance data narrows sigma-hat, earlier observations that were unremarkable under the wider limits computed at the time can retroactively read as beyond-3-sigma outliers relative to the new, tighter limits — process improvement can make history look anomalous in hindsight." Concretely: under EXPANDING_HISTORY, WECO/Nelson Rule 1 is clear for builds 1–14, then triggers continuously for builds 15 through 30 — in every one of those triggered builds, the flagged point is always build 1 (score 64), because it never leaves the expanding window. Under TRAILING_WINDOW (15), Rule 1 also triggers frequently, but the flagged point changes over time (build 8, then 10, then 23, then 24) and clears again by builds 29–30, once the offending point ages out of the 15-build window.

What I should say

"This is actually the most interesting result in Experiment A. It shows a real methodological trade-off, not a bug: expanding history gives you the most data, but it can let one old, no-longer-relevant build haunt every future control-limit calculation. A trailing window forgets old data on purpose, which costs you statistical power early on but keeps the chart honest about 'what does this process look like recently.' Neither method is simply 'more correct' — the experiment's value is making that trade-off visible with real numbers instead of leaving it as a textbook claim."

The Regression Spike at Build 23

Phase D ("Deliberate regression") begins exactly at build 23. The real generated data shows a genuine step-change, not just a documented intention: defectScore jumps from 7 (build 22, the last build of the stable Phase C) to 46 (build 23). src/spc/regressionSpikeDetection.ts's condition — moving range beyond its control limit and the score got worse — fires correctly at exactly this build.

Trend, CUSUM/Drift, and Control-Limit Behavior

  • Trend detection is exercised naturally by Phase B (builds 9–15, a genuine downward run) and Phase E (builds 26–30, recovery) — both are the kind of multi-build directional movement detectTrend() is built to catch, as distinct from the single-build shock at build 23.
  • CUSUM/drift is exercised by the same gradual phases — its whole purpose is catching a persistent lean that no single point-rule flags, which is exactly what Phase B and Phase E look like statistically.
  • Control-limit violations (the direct UCL/LCL comparison) fire whenever the latest point crosses the computed limit — most visibly at build 23, and repeatedly through Phase A while the process is still noisy and the limits are still wide.
Possible reviewer question

"Why did you design five phases instead of, say, three or ten?" Five is the minimum needed to exercise every qualitatively different scenario the gate policy has an opinion about: a bad starting state, genuine improvement, genuine stability, a genuine regression, and genuine recovery. Fewer phases would leave some detector untested; more would just add builds without adding a new statistical scenario.

Experiment B — Process Capability

Generator: scripts/generate-stable-cohort.ts + src/telemetry/stableProcessGenerator.ts, seed 71430901. Output: 30 records in telemetry/experiments/stable-capability-cohort/ (build-stable-001.json … build-stable-030.json). Verified by scripts/verify-stable-cohort.ts (30 checks).

Why a Separate Experiment?

Beginner explanation

Capability analysis (Cpu/Cpk) only means something if the process it's measuring is actually behaving consistently. Experiment A is deliberately not consistent — it has five different regimes on purpose. So instead of computing a capability number on that inconsistent data (which would be statistically meaningless), a second, separate cohort was built that is deliberately just one steady regime, with no phases, no trend, no regression baked in.

Technical explanation

Cpu = (USL − X̄) / (3σ̂). With a low, tightly-clustered defect score series (center line well under 40, and a small σ̂ from the tight moving ranges), the gap between the process average and the USL is large relative to the process's own spread — which is exactly what produces a Cpu comfortably above the 1.33 "capable" threshold. A Cpu near 3 says the process, as generated, sits roughly three times further from the specification limit than the minimum "capable" bar requires.

Why Cpk Was Excluded From Experiment A

This deserves its own section because it's the single most likely place a statistically literate reviewer will probe, and the honest answer is a real methodological argument, not an apology.

What I should say

"Cpk is not mathematically wrong to compute on Experiment A's data. The problem would be applying capability analysis to a deliberately non-stationary process — one I built to have five different regimes on purpose — and then interpreting the result as if it represented a single stable process. That interpretation would be the error, not the arithmetic. So I simply didn't compute it there. Experiment A never supplies a USL, and the capability field is null for every one of its 30 builds, by construction."

Experiment AExperiment B
RegimeFive distinct regimes (deliberately non-stationary)One regime (deliberately stationary)
USL supplied?No — capability.usl/cpu/capable are null for all 30 builds, in both analysis methodsYes — USL = 40
What it's useful forSPC baseline/window methodology — proving expanding-history and trailing-window control charts behave correctly across changing conditionsCapability analysis — proving Cpu/Cpk behaves correctly when its stability assumption actually holds
Verified byverify-experiment.ts check: "Cpk excluded from Experiment A" — asserts capability.usl/cpu/capable are null for every build/method, and that CPK_CAPABILITY never appears as a gate reasonverify-stable-cohort.ts's capability-integration checks, plus verify-dashboard.ts's Cpu≈2.9811 regression guard
Technical explanation

Process capability indices (Cp, Cpk, Cpu) are only meaningful under the assumption that the underlying process is in statistical control — a single, stable distribution. Computing Cpk on data that contains a known regime change (Phase C → Phase D) would report a number that describes an average across regimes that never actually coexisted, which is not what a capability index is supposed to mean. This is a standard caveat in SPC methodology, not a project-specific rule invented to dodge a hard question.

Possible reviewer criticism

"Isn't excluding Cpk from Experiment A just avoiding an inconvenient result?" No — and the strongest evidence against that reading is that Experiment B's own USL (40) is copied unchanged from Experiment A's context/ARCHITECTURE.md's example, specifically to avoid the appearance of picking a number that manufactures a favorable Cpu. If the goal had been to produce a flattering Cpk, the natural move would have been to tune the USL against Experiment A's own noisy data until something "capable" came out. Instead, a second, honestly-labeled experiment was built where the assumption actually holds.
